SESSION 06 · STUDY NOTES · EMBEDDING + TRANSFORMER INTRO

Text becomes a vector.

Three generations of text-to-number, from the dumb (CountVectorizer) to the smart-but-static (Word2Vec) to the smart-and-contextual (Transformer).

COUNTVEC → WORD2VEC → XFMR ATTENTION IS ALL YOU NEED · 2017 ENCODER + DECODER
WHERE WE ARE

Session 5 cleaned text — lowercase, tokenize, lemmatize, drop stopwords. The output was a list of cleaned tokens, not numbers yet. Today we close that gap: every token becomes a vector of floats, and we walk through three generations of how to do that — each generation solving problems the previous couldn't.

By the end of this session, you'll understand why the Transformer paper was a watershed in 2017, what it added to the embedding game, and what the encoder vs decoder blocks actually do. The architecture details come in Session 7 (already noted — the project's gold-standard reference). Tomorrow we start filling in the maths: positional encoding, then attention.

Three generations of text-to-number, each fixing the previous one's blind spot. CountVectorizer ignores meaning and counts words. Word2Vec captures meaning but is static — one fixed vector per word, no context, no position. Transformer captures meaning and position and relationships between words, producing a different vector for the same word in different sentences.
§ 01 — RECAP

Quick recap (and one terminology fix)

Session 5 produced a sequence of cleaned tokens after four steps: lowercase → tokenize → stem/lemmatize → drop stopwords. The example carried over:

Raw:     "The quick brown foxes ARE jumping over the lazy dogs!"
Cleaned: ["quick", "brown", "fox", "jump", "lazy", "dog"]
Correction (again) — "corpus" vs "vocabulary."

The lecturer used "corpus" here to mean "the set of unique cleaned tokens." That's still not the standard NLP usage. Quick reminder from Session 5: corpus = the entire collection of documents you're working with; vocabulary = the set of unique tokens that appear in your corpus. So "my corpus is 20,000 tokens" should be "my vocabulary is 20,000 tokens." This is the second session where the conflation happens; carry the corrected usage into interviews.

We're now going to take that list of cleaned tokens and turn each one into a vector of numbers. There are three generations of methods, in order of sophistication.

§ 02 — GENERATION 1

CountVectorizer: the dumb one

CountVectorizer builds a 2D matrix. Rows are documents (one row per review, comment, or sentence). Columns are tokens (one column per unique token in your vocabulary). The value at row i, column j is the number of times token j appears in document i.

Worked example

Two sentences:

Sentence 1: "quick brown fox jump"
Sentence 2: "dog jump dog learn fast"

Vocabulary across both: ["quick", "brown", "fox", "jump", "dog", "learn", "fast"] — 7 unique tokens.

The CountVectorizer matrix:

Documentquickbrownfoxjumpdoglearnfast
Sentence 11111000
Sentence 20001211

Sentence 1 becomes the vector [1, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0]. Sentence 2 becomes [0, 0, 0, 1, 2, 1, 1]. These are now numbers — a math model can multiply weights against them.

Where it works, where it doesn't

It works for: simple text classification on small vocabularies — bag-of-words sentiment classifiers, spam filters, topic categorisation. The classical scikit-learn recipe is CountVectorizer + LogisticRegression for sentiment, which is surprisingly competitive on short reviews.

It doesn't work for: anything that needs meaning. Two sentences with the same vocabulary but different meanings get similar vectors. Two sentences with the same meaning but different vocabulary get totally different vectors. There's no notion that "good" and "great" are similar.

TF-IDF (a half-step up)

TF-IDF (Term Frequency × Inverse Document Frequency) is a refinement of CountVectorizer. Instead of raw counts, each cell is weighted by how rare the token is across the entire corpus. A token that appears in every document gets weighted down; a token that appears in only a few documents gets weighted up.

This fixes the "the/is/and dominate the counts" problem for cases where you didn't drop stopwords. It's a single line of sklearn (TfidfVectorizer) and a standard baseline for any text classification task. The lecturer mentioned it in passing; for traditional NLP it's almost always TF-IDF, not raw CountVectorizer, in production.

JAVA/SPRING BRIDGE

CountVectorizer ≈ a SQL pivot table. Rows are records (documents), columns are categorical dimensions (tokens), values are aggregates (counts). It's a pivot. Same data shape you'd produce in BigQuery or PostgreSQL with GROUP BY document_id, token, COUNT(*) followed by a pivot.

§ 03 — GENERATION 2

Word2Vec: static-but-smart

CountVectorizer doesn't know that "good" and "great" are similar. Word2Vec (Mikolov et al. 2013) was the first widely-used method that did.

The output of Word2Vec is, for each token in your vocabulary, a fixed-length vector of floats — typically 100, 200, or 300 dimensions. The vectors are learned by training a small neural network on a large text corpus (Wikipedia, Google News) with a clever objective: predict a word given its neighbours (CBOW), or predict the neighbours given a word (Skip-gram).

The result has a remarkable property: words that appear in similar contexts end up with similar vectors. "King" and "queen" are close. "Apple" and "orange" are close. "Bank" (river) and "bank" (institution) — well, that's a problem, see §04.

The "topic dimensions" intuition

The lecturer drew the following picture:

Worddim 1 (animal)dim 2 (machine)dim 3 (fruit)dim 4 (sky)dim 5 (vegetable)
apple0.10.80.70.20.1
bird0.90.10.10.80.0
okra0.10.10.30.00.9

The intuition: each dimension represents a "topic" (animal, fruit, etc.); each word's vector tells you how strongly that word relates to each topic. "Apple" scores high on both machine (because of the company) and fruit, so it sits between those two regions in the vector space.

Correction — Word2Vec dimensions are not labelled topics.

The above table is a teaching metaphor, not how Word2Vec actually works. Real Word2Vec dimensions are uninterpretable latent dimensions — the training process doesn't label them, and inspecting a single dimension across all words doesn't reveal a coherent topic. The geometry of the whole vector space does encode meaning (similar words cluster, certain directions encode analogies), but individual axes are not aligned with human-interpretable concepts. Real interpretability research (NMF, sparse coding) actively works against the native Word2Vec axes precisely because they aren't meaningful. (This is the same correction the Session 7 notes already make for the encoder's embedding space.)

Geometry that actually works

What Word2Vec does give you, that's both real and impressive:

  1. Similar-words-cluster. Cosine similarity between vectors tracks human-judged word similarity reasonably well.
  2. Analogy via vector arithmetic. The classic result: vec("king") − vec("man") + vec("woman") ≈ vec("queen"). The vector space encodes gender, royalty, plurality, and other relationships as directions.
  3. Generalisation to unseen tasks. Drop Word2Vec vectors into a classifier and you get a useful baseline without any task-specific embedding training.

Visualising the high-dimensional space

Word2Vec vectors live in 100-300 dimensional space. You can't draw that. The lecturer used PCA (Principal Component Analysis) or t-SNE to project the 300D vectors down to 2D for plotting. The 2D plot is approximate — points that look close in 2D may not be close in the original 300D space — but it's the best you can do for visualisation.

Correction — Word2Vec wasn't quite "the latest before Transformer."

Word2Vec is 2013. GloVe (Pennington et al.) is 2014. Between Word2Vec/GloVe and the Transformer (2017), there was ELMo (Peters et al., February 2018) — the first widely-used contextualised embedding. ELMo used a bi-directional LSTM to produce different vectors for the same word in different contexts, which directly addressed the polysemy problem of static Word2Vec. ELMo set the stage for BERT (October 2018), which used a Transformer encoder for the same purpose. The story isn't "Word2Vec → Transformer"; it's "Word2Vec → GloVe → ELMo → BERT."

JAVA/SPRING BRIDGE

Word2Vec ≈ a Map<String, double[]> lookup. A pre-trained dictionary: input a word, get the same fixed-length vector every time. No context dependency. You can ship the pre-trained model as a binary file, load it with one line of gensim, and treat it as a constant lookup table for the rest of your pipeline. Same operational shape as a Spring @Cacheable service backed by an immutable map.

§ 04 — WHY WE NEEDED MORE

The three problems with static embeddings

Word2Vec was the state of the art for years (2013-2018), but it has three structural blind spots. Each became a motivation for the Transformer.

4.1 No context awareness

The lecturer's example:

"Ram is a good boy. He is going to school."

A model reading this needs to know that "he" refers to Ram. Word2Vec can't help — vec("he") is the same vector no matter what came before. The word's identity is fixed; it doesn't get "infused" with the meaning of the previous sentence.

This is called coreference resolution in classical NLP, and it's hard. With static embeddings, you'd need a separate coreference-resolution module bolted on top. The Transformer's attention mechanism handles it implicitly — by attending to "Ram" while processing "he," the model can produce a contextualised vector for "he" that effectively carries Ram's information.

4.2 Polysemy — one word, many meanings

"This apple is red. I bought the new Apple phone."

Both sentences contain "apple," but they refer to different things — a fruit and a company. Static Word2Vec assigns the same vector to "apple" in both cases. The vector is a blurry compromise — an average over all the training contexts in which "apple" appeared — so it sits awkwardly between "fruit" and "machine," helping neither task.

Correction — "apple sits between fruit and machine" is the lecturer's intuition, not a useful behaviour.

Word2Vec assigns one vector to "apple." That vector is the result of averaging across all contexts the word appeared in during training — fruit contexts, company contexts, the apple-of-my-eye idiom. The resulting vector is genuinely ambiguous. For downstream tasks this is a bug, not a feature: a sentiment classifier processing "I love apple pies" gets the same word vector as one processing "Apple's stock dropped." Modern Transformer embeddings fix this directly: the same surface word gets two completely different vectors in those two sentences.

4.3 Order-blindness

"bird sky fly" — "fly sky bird" — "bird fly sky"

Three different sentences. Under CountVectorizer, all three produce the same vector [1, 1, 1] (each word appears once). Under Word2Vec, if you take the average of the three word vectors (a common simple aggregation), all three sentences again produce the same average — because addition is commutative.

This is fatal for any task where order matters: translation, sentiment ("not good" vs "good not"), question answering. Word2Vec's vocabulary-level vectors carry no information about where each word sits in the sentence.

The fix has two parts in the Transformer:

  • Positional encoding adds a position-dependent signal to each token's vector.
  • Attention lets each token's final vector be influenced by which specific tokens came before/after it, not just their identities.

Both are covered in Session 7.

§ 05 — TERMINOLOGY

What is a Language Model? What is "LLM"?

The lecturer's working definition: a language model is a model that "understands language" — text or speech in any human language. The LLM (Large Language Model) variant is so-called because it has billions of parameters and was trained on huge amounts of data.

That's roughly right. The textbook-precise definition:

A language model is a probability distribution over sequences of tokens. Given a sequence t₁, t₂, …, tₙ, it tells you P(tₙ | t₁, …, tₙ₋₁) — the probability of the next token given everything before it.

That single mechanism — "what's the most likely next token?" — is what every modern LLM (GPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama) does at its core. Everything else (chat, function calling, RAG, agents) is a wrapper around repeated next-token prediction.

Correction — "Large" in LLM refers to size and data, not just parameters.

The lecturer framed "large" as a function of parameter count. That's half the picture. The original GPT-3 paper (Brown et al. 2020) made "large" concrete by scaling both parameters (175 billion) and training data (300 billion tokens) together, observing that capability emerged from the combination. The "scaling laws" (Kaplan et al. 2020, Hoffmann et al. 2022) describe this as a coupled relationship — for a given compute budget, there's an optimal ratio of parameters to data. So "large" is really "large in compute," with parameters and data growing together.

Pre-trained vs fine-tuned vs from-scratch

ModeWhat you doWhen
Pre-trained, use as-isDownload Llama, call OpenAI's API.95% of agentic-AI work.
Fine-tunedTake a pre-trained model, continue training on your domain data.When the model needs your company's jargon, format, or behaviour.
From scratchTrain all weights yourself on a huge corpus.When you're a foundation lab (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Mistral).

This session, and the rest of the course, focuses on mode 1 — you call APIs, embed text using existing models, build agents on top.

§ 06 — THE WATERSHED

Transformer: what changed in 2017

The paper: "Attention Is All You Need" — Vaswani, Shazeer, Parmar, Uszkoreit, Jones, Gomez, Kaiser, Polosukhin — Google Brain & Google Research, June 2017. NeurIPS 2017. Cited 100,000+ times.

What it introduced, in three claims:

  1. Self-attention as a sequence-modelling primitive. Replace recurrence (RNNs, LSTMs) entirely with a mechanism where every token attends to every other token in the sequence. This makes the architecture massively parallel on GPUs — RNNs process one token at a time; Transformers process the whole sequence simultaneously.
  2. Positional encoding to add order information to the otherwise order-agnostic attention. Sin/cos waves at different frequencies; element-wise added (not concatenated) to the input embeddings.
  3. The encoder-decoder structure with N=6 stacked blocks each, joined by cross-attention between encoder and decoder. The encoder consumes input; the decoder generates output.
Correction — Transformer didn't cause "exponential growth in AI" by itself.

The lecturer attributed the AI explosion to the Transformer alone. The honest version: the Transformer was the algorithmic unlock, but the explosion required three other things in parallel — GPU scale (NVIDIA A100s, H100s, then B200s), data scale (Common Crawl, the Pile, RedPajama), and infrastructure investment (hundreds of billions of dollars from Microsoft, Google, Meta, Amazon, OpenAI, Anthropic). Strip out any one of those legs and the curve flattens. Crediting attention alone misses the systems-and-economics half of the story.

§ 07 — TWO BLOCKS

Encoder vs Decoder at a glance

The original Transformer paper has two blocks. They look almost identical but do different jobs.

BlockJobInputOutput
EncoderBuild a rich contextualised representation of the inputTokens of the source (input)A vector per input token, embedding position + meaning + relationships
DecoderGenerate output tokens one at a time, attending to the encoder's representationEncoder output + tokens generated so farProbability distribution over the next token

The slogan from the lecturer's recap:

"Encoder outputs embeddings. Decoder outputs generations."

That's accurate. The encoder's job ends with a vector. The decoder consumes that vector (via cross-attention) and produces a sequence of tokens. The decoder is the part that actually "writes" the response.

Correction — "encoder is the middleman that translates human language to alien numbers."

The lecturer's alien analogy is cute but slightly off. The encoder doesn't translate text to numbers — by the time text reaches the encoder, the tokenizer has already turned it into token IDs, and the embedding layer has already turned each ID into a vector (looked up from a learned table). The encoder's job is to take those initial vectors and produce contextualised vectors — same dimensionality, but now each vector reflects information about every other token in the sequence. The encoder is a transform, not a tokenizer.

Correction — decoder predicts the next token, not the next word.

A word like "preprocessing" might be 3 BPE tokens ("pre", "process", "##ing"). The decoder predicts one of those at a time, then conditions on its own previous predictions to predict the next one. After enough token-by-token generation, the model has produced text. This is why API providers bill per token, not per word.

JAVA/SPRING BRIDGE

Encoder ≈ read-side service, decoder ≈ write-side service (CQRS-ish). The encoder takes an input request and produces a canonical structured representation; the decoder takes that representation and produces an output stream. Like the read and write sides of a CQRS-style system, both blocks operate on the same canonical data type (vectors here, events there) but with different roles. Structurally symmetric — both are Transformer blocks built from the same primitives — but the semantics differ.

§ 08 — TAXONOMY

Encoder-only, decoder-only, encoder-decoder

The original 2017 paper put encoder and decoder together for machine translation (English ↔ German). But subsequent research showed you can use just one of the two blocks, depending on the task.

ArchitectureExample modelsBest forWhy
Encoder-onlyBERT, RoBERTa, DistilBERTClassification, NER, embedding generation, retrievalThe encoder produces rich contextualised vectors; perfect for "understand this input."
Decoder-onlyGPT-3/4/5, Claude, Llama, Mistral, DeepSeek, GemmaGeneration, chat, agents, code completionThe decoder is built to predict tokens one at a time; the entire LLM-as-chatbot world is decoder-only.
Encoder-decoderT5, BART, original Transformer, WhisperTranslation, summarisation, speech-to-textThe encoder digests the input; the decoder produces a different output. Useful when input and output are different "languages."

Use-case mapping

  • Named Entity Recognition (NER) — "find all people, places, dates in this text" → encoder only. The output is per-input-token labels, not new text. BERT-NER is the standard.
  • Text generation / chat — "write a response to this prompt" → decoder only. GPT/Claude/Llama.
  • Translation — "translate Hindi to English" → encoder-decoder. The two languages are different "tongues"; the encoder digests Hindi, the decoder produces English.
  • Text embedding — "give me a vector for this sentence so I can do retrieval" → encoder only. Examples: OpenAI's text-embedding-3-* models, Sentence-BERT, Cohere's embedding models.
Correction — BERT vs GPT, more precisely.

BERT (Devlin et al. 2018) is bidirectional: each token's encoder representation attends to every other token, both left and right. This makes it great for understanding but unable to generate new text (it has no notion of "previous token only"). GPT (Radford et al. 2018) is autoregressive: each token attends only to tokens before it in the sequence (enforced by masked attention, the one architectural difference between encoder and decoder blocks). That left-to-right constraint is what makes the decoder a generator — it can predict "what comes next" because that's how it was trained.

§ 09 — DEPTH

Stacking and scale

The original 2017 paper used N=6 identical encoder blocks stacked on top of each other, and N=6 decoder blocks. Each block contains the full attention + feed-forward + residual + LayerNorm structure (Session 7 covers the block contents).

Six is not magic. Modern models go much deeper:

ModelEncoder/Decoder layersHidden dimParameters
Original Transformer (2017)6 + 6512~65M
BERT-base12768110M
BERT-large241024340M
GPT-396 (decoder only)12288175B
Llama 3 70B80 (decoder only)819270B
Llama 3.1 405B126 (decoder only)16384405B

The deeper the stack, the more "passes" each token's representation gets refined through self-attention. Performance and capability scale with depth (and width, and data) — that's the "scaling laws" finding from §05.

SIDE NOTE

N is a hyperparameter, not an architectural constant. When you read code or papers and see n_layers, num_hidden_layers, or num_blocks, that's N. It's a choice. The "6 in the paper" comes from what fit on the hardware Vaswani had available in 2017.

Generative AI, briefly

The lecturer placed Transformer inside the broader generative AI family. Worth a one-line tour:

Correction — the lecturer skipped diffusion models.

Her list mentioned GANs, VAEs, flow-based, RNN, and Transformer. Diffusion models were missing — and they're the dominant image-generation approach since ~2022. DALL-E 2 (April 2022), Stable Diffusion (August 2022), Midjourney, Imagen, Flux are all diffusion-based, not GAN-based. If you read about modern image AI, you're reading about diffusion almost exclusively.

Correction — "GANs are for deepfakes, outdated."

The lecturer framed GANs as relics. Not quite. GANs are still actively used for certain image-synthesis tasks (the StyleGAN family for face generation; CycleGAN for style transfer). What's true: diffusion has displaced GANs as the default choice for new image-generation projects since 2022. GANs are no longer the SOTA, but they're not dead.

§ NEXT — SESSION 07

For Session 7

Tomorrow's topic is the encoder block in detail — three things:

  1. Input embedding — turn each input token into a vector (we already understand this; it's a learned lookup table, like Word2Vec but trained jointly with the rest of the model).
  2. Positional encoding — add a position-dependent signal to each embedding so the model knows token i came before token j. The original paper used sin/cos waves at different frequencies; modern models often use RoPE (Rotary Position Embedding) or ALiBi (Attention with Linear Biases) instead.
  3. Self-attention — every token's representation is updated based on a weighted combination of every other token's representation. This is the mechanism that lets "he" attend to "Ram" three sentences earlier.

Plus the supporting structure that holds it together:

  • Multi-head attention — run attention multiple times in parallel with different learned projections, then concatenate.
  • Feed-forward sublayer — a small ANN applied to each position (this is the "feed-forward" in the diagram).
  • Residual connections + LayerNorm — make the deep stack trainable.

Session 7's existing notes (Session7-TransformerEncoder/Session7-TransformerEncoder-notes-v2.html) already cover all of this with the project's canonical layout — it's the gold-standard reference. Treat tomorrow's class as an in-depth walkthrough of what's already noted there.

Correction — positional encoding is added, not concatenated.

Quick flag because it's a common point of confusion: in the original Transformer, the positional encoding vector is element-wise added to the input embedding vector (both have the same dimensionality, e.g. 512). Adding lets each dimension carry both content and positional information simultaneously. Concatenating would double the dimensionality and treat position as a separate "channel," which the architects rejected. Modern variants (RoPE, ALiBi) integrate position differently again — but they're all variations on "fold position into the existing vector," not "tack position on as extra dimensions."

INTERVIEW-READY SUMMARY

"Three generations of text embedding. CountVectorizer is just word counts in a matrix — no notion of meaning. Word2Vec (Mikolov 2013) produces a learned static vector per word — similar words cluster, but it has no context awareness, no polysemy handling, no order. The Transformer (Vaswani 2017) fixes all three by combining a learned embedding layer with positional encoding and self-attention; the same word gets a different vector in different sentences. The Transformer has two halves — encoder for understanding, decoder for generation. BERT is encoder-only, GPT/Llama/Claude are decoder-only, T5/BART are encoder-decoder. Choice depends on task — encoder for classification or NER, decoder for chat or generation, both for translation."

Glossary

Attention
A mechanism by which each token's representation is updated based on a weighted combination of every other token's representation. The core innovation of the Transformer. Detailed in Session 7+.
BERT
Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers. The canonical encoder-only model (Devlin et al. 2018). Used for classification, NER, embedding.
CountVectorizer
The simplest text-to-number method: a matrix of token counts per document. Available in scikit-learn.
Cross-attention
Attention between the decoder and the encoder's output. Lets the decoder consult the encoded input while generating.
Decoder
The block of a Transformer that generates output tokens autoregressively. Uses masked self-attention so each position only attends to earlier positions.
Diffusion model
A generative model that learns to reverse a noising process, used dominantly for image generation since 2022. Examples: Stable Diffusion, DALL-E 2/3, Midjourney.
ELMo
Embeddings from Language Models (Peters et al. 2018). The first widely-used contextualised embedding, bridging Word2Vec and BERT.
Encoder
The block of a Transformer that produces a contextualised representation of the input. Bidirectional attention; outputs vectors, not tokens.
Encoder-decoder
A Transformer architecture with both blocks, joined by cross-attention. Used for sequence-to-sequence tasks (translation, summarisation).
GAN (Generative Adversarial Network)
A generative model architecture using two competing networks (generator + discriminator). Pre-2022, the default for image generation; now displaced by diffusion.
GloVe
Global Vectors for Word Representation (Pennington et al. 2014). A static word embedding, alternative to Word2Vec.
GPT
Generative Pre-trained Transformer (Radford et al. 2018, 2019, Brown et al. 2020). The canonical decoder-only model.
Language Model
A probability distribution over sequences of tokens. Given previous tokens, predicts the next.
LLM (Large Language Model)
A language model with billions of parameters trained on huge amounts of text. Modern LLMs are all decoder-only Transformers.
Masked attention
Attention where future positions are zeroed out, so each token only attends to itself and earlier tokens. Required for autoregressive generation.
Multi-head attention
Running attention multiple times in parallel with different learned projections, then concatenating the results.
Positional encoding
A position-dependent vector added to each token's embedding so the model knows where each token sits in the sequence. Original Transformer used sin/cos waves; modern variants include RoPE and ALiBi.
Polysemy
A word having multiple meanings (e.g., "bank" as river bank vs financial institution). Static embeddings (Word2Vec) can't distinguish; contextualised embeddings (Transformer) can.
Self-attention
Attention where the query, key, and value all come from the same sequence. The basic mechanism in both encoder and decoder.
t-SNE
A dimensionality reduction technique used to visualise high-dimensional embeddings in 2D or 3D.
TF-IDF
Term Frequency × Inverse Document Frequency. A refinement of CountVectorizer that weights tokens by rarity across documents.
Transformer
The 2017 architecture (Vaswani et al.) based on self-attention. Foundation for all modern LLMs.
Word2Vec
A static word embedding (Mikolov et al. 2013). One fixed vector per word; first widely-used method capturing semantic similarity.
